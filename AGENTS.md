# Project rules for agents

Claude is the only agent working on DeanHack; Codex was retired on 2026-09-24. Any file in the repository may be edited, including `prototype/engine/bridge.c`, `prototype/engine/server.js` and gameplay code in `src/`.

Agents work in the **Claude worktree**, a sibling folder named `deanhack-claude` next to the user's **play copy** (the folder named `deanhack`). Never work in the play copy. The exact locations on each machine are in the table in `CLAUDE.md`.

Keep the **handoff log** (`deanhack-handoff.md` in the root of the play copy) up to date. Always use that copy, never the one inside another worktree. The log is untracked (in `.gitignore`): append to it, but never `git add` or commit it. It is the running record the user and scheduled runs read:
- Before starting, read the recent entries.
- When finishing, run the relevant tests and builds, commit on a `claude/...` branch, and append the files changed, checks run, commit hash and open concerns.

Do not retune `LIVE_AMBIENT`, `TORCH_INTENSITY`, or lantern lighting without documenting the reason in the handoff log. These settings are deliberate contrast fixes.

The user's Live game runs from the play copy. Never send input to it for testing. Use the worktree's own engine, or `prototype/engine/smoke.py` and its separate character.

Merge one branch at a time. After a merge, update the working branch from `origin/master` before beginning another task. Before a session ends, list its open `claude/*` PRs and confirm each is still mergeable: the routines squash-merge to `master` on their schedule and most merges touch `AGENTS.md`, so a PR left waiting can conflict before long and auto-merge gives up without a word (the level-2 shop PR sat six hours this way on 2026-10-11). Merged branches are swept with `prototype/tools/sweep-branches.sh --delete` (run locally with `gh`; a cloud session cannot delete refs), and the repository setting "Automatically delete head branches" keeps new ones from piling up.

**Cloud sessions** (claude.ai/code) run in their own checkout with no access to the play copy, so they cannot read or append the handoff log and have no `gh`: for them the PR body is the handoff entry (files changed, checks run, open concerns), and auto-merge is set through the GitHub tools. Say in the PR body that it was a cloud session.

## Art direction

The player trudges through a dark, barren, hostile fantasy world full of monsters. It is not baking tea cakes. Lean into sinister, sharp and menacing: harsh angular silhouettes, worn, scarred, grimy materials, desaturated earth tones with sickly accents, blood, bone, rust, ash and rot. Never make things rounder, softer, brighter, cuter, pastel, bubbly or fairy-tale. If something looks cute, make it grim.

- **Models** are rooted in common-sense fantasy tropes: things look like what a player expects (a dragon looks like a dragon, an altar like a place of sacrifice).
- **Wand materials:** every wand appearance in `src/objects.c` now has its own look (`wand.js`, `wand.test.js`), including plastic, bone, electrum, titanium, nickel, mithril, orichalcum, alabaster, grooved and bent. The short wand is a chipped stub (`nicks` in `wand.js`). The pine and mahogany woods are now knotted and pitted, the curved wand is blackened iron, maple is grain-veined and balsa chipped. Every wood now has a tell.
- **Spellbooks:** give spellbooks their own visual tells, so each cover looks distinct: leather, cloth, vellum, metal or bone, clasps and corner fittings, stitched or branded glyphs, tarnish and water stains, and a school colour from the Visual language palette on its glyph or edge.
- **Unseen monster marker:** a creature the hero cannot see (invisible, without see invisible) shows in the ASCII game as a grey capital I, so you know something is there. The bridge already sends `invisible` on the cell. Draw a clear marker for it: a grey capital I with a faint shimmering distortion of the air, readable at a glance and plainly not a real monster.
- **Animations** lead into whimsy: personality, odd tics and surprise in how things move. The whimsy lives in the motion, not the look, so keep it dark-humoured and a little uncanny, never cute or bouncy.

### The style in ten lines

Our roguelike in short. When in doubt, check a change against these.

1. **Dark, barren and hostile.** The dungeon wants you dead. Rust, ash, bone, rot, grime; never cute, bubbly, pastel or fairy-tale.
2. **Fantasy tropes, honestly drawn.** A dragon looks like a dragon and an altar like an altar. Things look like what a player expects, then a little worse.
3. **The game is shown, not told.** Visuals carry the meaning; text and chrome stay minimal. No audio, ever.
4. **Silhouette first.** Readable from the top-down camera at a few tiles across. Angular, faceted, tapered; never smooth balls, capsules or toys.
5. **Magic is loud against the dark.** Magic items are the player's lifeline and the strongest things in the world: saturated, self-lit, punchy colour and a clear effect. Mundane things stay dull. Rarer and stronger means grander.
6. **Leaks are welcome.** A sharp-eyed player may read an item's true nature from its look. A little hint is a reward.
7. **Threat has presence.** The more dangerous a thing is, the bigger, richer and more menacing it is: glow, size, detail, wrongness. Bosses and killers are never plain.
8. **Everything is worn.** Scuffs, dents, stains, asymmetry, a torn ear or chipped horn. Nothing is factory clean, nothing is perfectly symmetrical, except the sacred and the strange.
9. **Motion has dark whimsy.** Animations carry personality, odd tics and surprise, dark-humoured and a little uncanny; never bouncy or sugary. Every state (blind, stunned, burning, dying) is unmistakable on screen.
10. **Convention for the player.** Follow roguelike habits so veterans feel at home: blessed is green, cursed is red, the hero is a Valkyrie in end-game steel. Judge by looking at the render at the game camera, against the benchmarks (the enormous rat, the weeping angels).

### Model quality bar

The user judges models at the game camera (top-down, a few tiles across), and has rejected faces and hands that were round, flat or toy-like. Hold every model to this bar, and check your work against it before you open a PR.

- **Benchmarks:** the enormous rat, the weeping angels and the High-threat monsters are the quality to match. Before changing a model, open one benchmark and one comparable model in `item-review.html`, and say in the PR body what you matched.
- **Silhouette first.** The outline must read at game-camera size: distinct shapes for head, shoulders, hands, weapon and tail, with gaps between them. If you cannot tell what it is from a black silhouette, adding detail will not save it.
- **Angular, not blobby.** Build heads, hands, limbs and bodies from faceted, tapered, slightly asymmetric forms (low-sided cylinders and cones, wedge cheeks and jaws, stepped plates), not smooth spheres, capsules and ellipsoids stacked together. A sphere is for an eyeball or a pearl, nothing else.
- **Proportion is realistic, not chibi.** No oversized heads, stubby limbs or mitten hands, no cartoon eyes. A humanoid head is about one seventh of the height; hands are fingers and a thumb, not paddles; wrists and ankles are thin. Anything cute gets grimmer.
- **Faces:** the features sit in shadow and light, not in outline. Deep-set eyes under a brow ridge, a nose bridge that catches light, cheekbones, a hard jaw, a small mouth. Eyes are small and bright against a darkened socket. Use value contrast (darker sockets, lighter ridges), not just a different colour. A face that reads as a flat disc, a mask or a doll is a failure.
- **Hands and held things:** every hand shows a thumb and at least four fingers (or claws) wrapped believably around what it holds, with knuckles, wrists and cuffs. Weapons and shields are held at the right point, not floating.
- **Detail hierarchy:** big shapes first, then medium (plates, straps, folds), then small (rivets, scratches, wear). Put the detail where the eye lands (head, hands, weapon, chest). Wear every surface: scuffs, dents, grime, rust, stains. Nothing is factory clean.
- **Material and value range:** use at least three tones per model (dark recesses, a mid body, a few bright accents such as metal edges, eyes or a glow). Avoid one flat colour and avoid pastel. Metals get roughness variation, cloth and leather look worn.
- **Asymmetry and character:** one chipped horn, a torn cloak, a mismatched pauldron, a scar. Perfect symmetry looks like a toy.
- **Cost:** merge geometry and reuse materials (see Performance). Do not buy detail with draw calls.
- **Verify by looking:** `item-review.html` and the demo scene render in Chromium through Playwright (`render-smoke.test.js` shows how); render the model at the game camera distance and at close range, and look at it before declaring it done. If it would look at home on a toy shelf, redo it.
- **Which model is the hero?** The live hero is `knight()` in `prototype/main.js`, with her face in `hero-face.js` (a sculpted, vertex-painted skull plus nose, eyes and brows) and her gauntlet hands in `hero-hand.js`. The Valkyrie's own suit (closed winged helm, crimson hair, riveted blackened plate over mail, after the user's reference photo of 2026-10-24) is `hero-valkyrie.js`, shown only by her kit, which hides the face behind the helm; every other class still shows the face. The helm's wings are half the photo's size (`WING_SIZE`; the user found them too tall) and show only while she wears a helmet (`syncHelmWings` in `knight()`, called by `setHelmet` and again by `dressHero` after it shows her suit). `valkyrie.js` is the Valkyrie *monster* (player-monster) model, not the hero: edits there do not change the hero. To see the hero, run the dev server and `OUT=/dir/ node prototype/tools/hero-shot.mjs name` (uses the `?debug` hook), then look at the images. The hero's face and hands were rebuilt this way and set the bar for heads and hands.

### Feedback loop and lessons

Every run both reads and feeds this section, so quality compounds instead of resetting each hour.

**Before you start (2 minutes):** read "Lessons so far" below, then list the last ten merged PRs. Any later commit or PR from a non-routine branch (`claude/*` that is not `claude/routine-*`) that touches the same file is a *correction*: the user or a Claude session found something wrong. Read its diff and message, and do not repeat the mistake it fixed.

**In your PR body, add a "Lessons" section** with two short parts: *Matched* (which benchmark you compared to, per the quality bar) and *Next run should know* (one sentence, or "nothing new"). A model PR also says how it was looked at: `tools/creature-shot.mjs` or `tools/item-shot.mjs` output, or `item-review.html` in Playwright. If a run cannot render, it says so there and limits itself to wear details on an existing model; a new or reshaped model that nobody looked at does not merge.

**Add a line to "Lessons so far" only when** you hit a reusable pitfall you verified (a failed test that taught you something, a model that looked wrong in the render, a convention you had to look up). One line, imperative, under 25 words, no duplicates, and delete or merge lines that overlap. Keep the list under 25 lines; if it is full, replace the weakest line. Do not record one-off facts about a single model.

#### Lessons so far

- *user* Judge models by their render at the game camera, not by tests passing. Tests that pass on a lump still ship a lump.
- *user* The enormous rat and the weeping angels are the benchmark: faceted, worn, asymmetric, with a clear silhouette. Match them.
- *user* Faces and hands must be sculpted (brow ridge, cheekbones, thumb plus four fingers); round, flat or toy-like ones get rejected.
- *user* Magic items must be loud: saturated, self-lit, with a floor glow. Potions, wands, spellbooks and scrolls on the floor were "SUPER underwhelming" when plain.
- *user* Blessed is green, cursed is red, everywhere (inventory rows, auras). Follow roguelike habits.
- *user* A pet or monster that is round, soft or pastel is wrong. Make it grim, then check it is still readable.
- *user* Collars, belts, straps and bands must wrap the body; assert `surfaceGap(part, body)` (`fit-check.js`) is small. The pet-cat collar floated for five PRs.
- Tests must feed what the bridge really sends: object classes are `oc_class` from `include/objclass.h` (strange object is 1, not 0). A class-0 test shipped a crate no shop showed.
- Held weapons point forward (+z) at the hand unless deliberately shouldered; assert the tip is ahead of the butt in a test (see `kobold.test.js`).
- The live hero is `knight()` in `main.js`; `valkyrie.js` is a monster. Find which factory the live game actually calls (`creatureFactory`, `live.js`) before editing a model.
- Colours passed to sculpt helpers must be hex, not arrays, or the result renders white.
- Adding glow, rune or skull meshes changes draw counts and bounds; update the draw-count and bounds tests on purpose, never loosen them.
- `prototype/package.json` runs tests by glob (`*.test.js`, `engine/*.test.js`): a new test file is picked up by itself, nothing to register, nothing to conflict on. A test file that is never meant to run has no place in those folders.
- A PR you leave open can conflict with the next merge (AGENTS.md most often); re-merge `master` and check auto-merge before you stop.
- *user* Small and safe is not the same as valuable: days of scars and 25 PRs on the cat added little and left the collar floating. Take a listed item toward its Done when, or open no PR.
- Time a frame by the gap between drawn frames, not by the time inside `render()`: the GPU runs behind the CPU, so `render()` returned in 10 to 30 ms on a software renderer that drew a frame every 1.2 s (`frameCost` in `quality.js`).
- Dark steel at metalness .8 renders black in the dungeon light (no environment map): keep metalness near .5, raise the value, and look at the render.
- Fidget seeds come from `g.id` (`ant-jaws.js`, `locust-hop.js`, `dangle.js`), so a model gaining an object shifts them and flips seed-sensitive tests; tests pin `g.userData.fidgetId`.
- Building or posing a creature's skin, joints or behaviour: read "What the pet cat and the dragons taught" in the Creature behaviour plan first.

### No audio

This game has no sound and will not get any. Do not add audio, sound effects, music or audio libraries, and do not propose them. Every cue (a step-over, a pickup, a hit, a status effect, a high-threat monster coming into view) must work visually.

### Orientation

Things are not always facing the way you would expect. The game moved from a flat ASCII world into 3D, and the translation from the engine's map directions to 3D facing is not always consistent. Known symptoms: monsters facing the wrong way, held weapons at the wrong angle, doors 90 degrees off in a doorway, and rolling boulder traps with the wrong orientation. Treat orientation as a real source of bugs. Whenever you add or touch a model, door, trap, weapon or animation, check which way it faces and which way it moves, and make that match what a player expects.

We want one standard convention for objects, traps, doors and weapons so facing stays consistent in feel, not a pile of per-object fixes. If you catch yourself adding a per-object rotation fudge (an offset such as a quarter turn), stop and ask whether the shared convention should be fixed instead.

### High-threat monsters

Anything with a warning level of 4 or above, and every demon, demon lord, demon prince and devil, needs special attention to detail and a lot more respect. These are the things that end the player. A balrog, for instance, is a maiar-level being incarnate with the power to end you, and it must look and feel like one: size, silhouette, glow, menace. Give them richer detail than ordinary monsters: more parts, more material variety, signature effects (evil glow, embers, smoke, heat), and wings that are large, bat-like and torn with holes. Never cute, never small, never a plain blob.

### Performance

Many routines add glows, motes, decals and effects, so keep a busy level smooth. Reuse geometry and materials, cap particle and decal counts, prefer cheap emissive materials to extra lights, and never add a per-frame cost that scales with everything on the map. The frame itself is paced by `quality.js` (2026-10-15): three tiers (high, balanced, low) set the pixel-ratio cap, SSAO, shadow size and the idle frame rate (30 fps after 2.5 s with no input, engine event or camera move); balanced is the default, F8 cycles and saves, `?quality=` overrides. Anything animated must look right at 30 fps too. The tiles' animated parts (sconce flames, trap sigils) are found once per tile by `tile-animations.js`, not by a walk of every tile each frame; a hook added to a tile after it is built needs `animated.add(tile)` again (dressFeature does). Every monster and pet is baked as it is staged (`bakeDraws` in `creatures.js`, called from `live.js`): meshes merge across plain materials per body part, with colour, roughness and metalness written per vertex (`baked-material.js`; glowing, textured, transparent and vertex-coloured pieces stay separate). The bestiary went from 6,828 to 4,180 meshes that way with no visible change, so a model's draw count in the game is what `bakeDraws` leaves, not what the builder makes; `perf-budget.test.js` and the contact-sheet tool measure the baked model. Builders and their tests still see the pieces they made. A mesh a builder animates through a closure (not an actor field or `g.userData`) would merge away: expose it, as `handles()` keeps those. Every plain part left after the merge is converted where it stands to the same baked material, and `batched-actors.js` then draws all baked parts of all monsters on the level from one `THREE.BatchedMesh` per shading variant (a room of monsters is a few draws, whatever their count); a part's own mesh stays in the rig as a proxy on a hidden layer and its instance follows it every frame. Effects need no changes: swapping a part's material (fade, ghosting, stone) makes it draw itself until the material is back, and rewriting its vertices (hem sway, writhe) drops it from the batch for good. A new effect that must tint one monster should clone the material (as those do), never write to the shared one. The level's repeated pieces (floor slabs, wall blocks, pebbles) draw as instances the same way (`tile-instances.js`: one `InstancedMesh` per geometry-and-material pair, proxies kept in the tile on a hidden layer, re-synced after each applied frame), so a new tile piece built from a shared geometry and one of three's own materials is instanced for free; give a piece a name or userData to keep it out. The pet cat is the one skinned creature (`feline-skin.js`, 2026-10-16): the feline parts merged into a SkinnedMesh over a 24-bone skeleton, animated by clips on a THREE.AnimationMixer (walk and idle cross-fade, the tail plays under both) the way three's skinning examples do; a skinned mesh does not batch, and the rigid cats' stalk and pounce code does not apply to it. Extend it by adding a pose function to `CLIPS`, not by posing bones per frame; the head stays with the glance code. Stay inside the budgets `perf-budget.test.js` checks (meshes and materials per monster model and per screenful of 30, no lights or particle layers on models, floor items and terrain capped too); raise a cap only with a reason in the PR body.

Work queues for the scheduled routines. ### Creature behaviour plan (both improvement routines; the daily review judges it)

The pet cat poses from goals: `feline-pose.js` solves "mouth at the paw", "rump and hind feet on the floor", "tail wrapped round the body on the floor" on whatever body it is given, and `feline-rest.js` holds the poses. The user wants every monster that has a body to get the same: behaviour written once by **body role** and solved per species, then each body region tuned to a real animal measured from references. Dragons first, then the monster families in the game's own order (`tools/creature-names.json`, which follows `src/monst.c`).

**How a routine works this plan.** Stages are marked **[M]** (the models routine: joints, a model change rendered and looked at), **[A]** (the animations and UI routine: role maps, poses and behaviour) or **[M or A]** (either routine: the reference measurements, so neither routine sits idle waiting on the other). Take the first open stage of your mark (an [M or A] stage counts as both), top to bottom, whose "needs" are merged on master. One stage per PR. When a stage is done, write under it "Done (routine, date, PR URL): ..." in the same PR; never delete stages, so the next run can see what came before. When every stage of a family is done, replace the family's stages with one "Done" line.
- *One subject, one touch* never blocks this plan: every stage is part of this one item, so both routines may keep working on dragons, or on one family, on consecutive runs.
- *Three strikes* counts runs on the same stage: three runs that fail one stage move that stage (not the plan) to the Claude list with a note, and the routines go on to the next open stage.
- *Never change the pet cat:* `feline-pose.test.js`, `feline-skin.test.js` and `feline-sculpt.test.js` pass unchanged at every stage.
- *Every stage is rendered:* a joints or pose stage is not done until `tools/creature-shot.mjs` (add a `?pose=<name>` hook to the shot tool the first time a pose needs one) has rendered it at the game camera and close up and the routine has looked at the result. The daily review renders the same.
- New files need no registering (tests run by glob). Measured numbers go in `prototype/CREATURE-MEASURES.md` (one section per family, every number with a working source link) and `creature-measures.js` (the constants, with a test of plausible ranges).

**What the pet cat and the dragons taught (read before any family's stage; Claude adds to it as families land).** These were each learned the hard way, with the user's eye or a failing render:
- *Look, every time.* Judge a skin or a pose by its render (`creature-shot`, three-quarter, side and game camera), not by tests passing. The user caught every cat flaw by eye: the sitting back's sharp angle, ears floating at the head's edges, eyes too big and too far apart, a chest puffed up too proud, feet sticking out of a sitting body, a tail through the floor, a sleeping tail curled the wrong way. Their own reference photos fixed more than anything else; ask for them when a pose or a face will not come right.
- *One skin, not a pile of parts.* An organic body is one surface (the cat: signed-distance pieces blended with smooth unions and meshed, `feline-hull.js`), so parts are seated in it and nothing floats. A body built of separate primitives (the dragons) is merged into skinned meshes per material instead.
- *Bend by skinning, never by nesting meshes.* Bones along a chain, each vertex weighted to its two nearest bones (`dragonSkin` in `creatures.js`), one `SkinnedMesh` per material: the chain bends smoothly and the mesh count does not rise. Nesting a mesh per joint doubled the dragons' meshes past the performance caps.
- *Everything on a chain rides it.* Spikes, spades, barbs, claws, fins and dressing are merged into the chain's skin (`skin.ride`); animated groups are hung on the nearest bone. Then bend every joint of every member in a test and check nothing stays behind (`dragon-follow.test.js`).
- *A long back or neck needs vertebrae.* One joint per region makes a kink; the cat's soft slope from shoulders to rump came from sharing each bend out along several vertebrae (`distribute` in `feline-skin.js`).
- *Poses from goals, by body role, not hand-set angles.* Write what the pose does ("chin on the left forepaw", "belly on the floor", "tail round the body toward the face") against role names (`body-roles.js`), and let `solvePose` find the angles on that body. Hand-set angles never survived a body change.
- *Solve in stages, and reach before you refine.* Body, head, wings, tail, then a final settle (`dragon-rest.js`). When goals fight (the chin's reach against a low neck), solve the reach first and add the shape goals from there, or the solver stalls halfway.
- *Say the shape, or the solver finds a silly one.* With only "chin on the paws" the dragon's neck arched up and hooked down; a cap on how far the neck rises, a face direction and "the head beside the body" made it curl like a cat.
- *Nothing through the floor: sample densely and at the end.* Sparse samples missed flat feet and a tail spade; a final settle stage over every second vertex caught them. Leave out what a later stage will lay (the tail) from earlier stages' floor checks, or it pins the body up.
- *A chain that leaves the body high has to ease down* (`drop` in `wrapTail`), and a tapering one lies on its own underside, measured from its skin, spade included.
- *Test geometry, not angles:* heights, distances, which side, what touches the floor. Angle tests broke every time the body changed.
- *At rest, a rig change must look exactly as before:* compare renders before and after (`compare -metric AE -fuzz 10%` gave 0 pixels), then render it bent (`BEND=`) and posed (`POSE=sleep`) and look.
- *Real numbers, honestly sourced.* Cite every measure; where no source gives an angle, write the posture in words and set it by eye against references. Never invent a number.
- *Solving is slow (1 to 12 s a form):* bake it ahead of time (`tools/bake-dragon-sleep.mjs` writes `dragon-sleep-poses.js`), gate each baked pose on its goals, and keep a test that the bake still matches a fresh solve, so a body change that forgets to re-bake fails loudly.
- *Seat what is laid on a body, and test for floating pieces.* Ribs, plates, scars, horns and dressing placed at fixed offsets floated off the dragons wherever the body was narrower (or its low-poly facets lay inside the ideal shape); `seat` in `creatures.js` slides each in until it sinks into the hide, and `dragon-seat.test.js` splits every dragon into its separate pieces and fails on any more than 1.5 cm from the rest (measured both ways, surface to surface). Run the same check on any creature you build.
- *Give the solver more than one way to do it.* One pose can't fit every body: a short neck can't curl, pillar legs can't fold. Try poses in order (curl, front, sprawl) and keep the first that meets every goal; and try a stuck stage from a few starts (the wings).
- *Bake from what the game will actually build:* the same name, colour and options. A pose baked on a default-coloured dragon put the sarkany's element spikes 1.6 cm through the floor in play.
- *Layer a pose on top of live motion without leaving residue:* ease from whatever the frame wrote toward the pose, record what you changed, and take it back at the start of the next frame unless something else rewrote it (`dragon-sleep.js`). Waking is then exact.

### Magic items

Give special attention to magic weapons, scrolls, rings, amulets, potions and wands, and to tools, above all the magical ones. In a dark, hostile world these are the things that carry wonder and awe, and the strong magic the player must harness to survive. Each magic item should radiate power in some way that is true to its effect, in both its model and its motion. Look for a unique, even whimsical, way to show what it does, so a player can feel the item's character before they use it.

The world gives a lot to draw on: a lamp that never runs dry, a wand of death that ends the most dangerous foe, a scroll that makes you vanish and reappear elsewhere, bolsters your defences, calls a fireball, floods a river, tames a monster or erases a whole species, an amulet that changes your sex or alignment or lets you fly, a ring that means you never go hungry or get grabbed, or that heals your wounds faster.

- **Models:** build the effect into the object's look: materials, glow, runes, wear, silhouette. Different items of the same class (two rings, two wands) should look different from each other, and each should hint at what it does.
- **Animations:** let the item's power show in how it moves and behaves: idles, pulses, flickers, drifting motes, and a clear, satisfying beat when it is used.
- **Pickup scoop:** the swell-and-motes moment belongs to the actual pickup. It tells the player the item made it into the pack, and it reads best with auto-pickup on. Walking over an item without picking it up triggers nothing.
- **Floor presence:** a magic item lying on the floor should catch the eye from across a room: a steady glow, shimmer or slow pulse in its own colour, with a faint halo on the ground. Rarer and more powerful items present more strongly, and artifacts the strongest of all. Mundane items stay dull so the magic stands out.
- **Pickup:** the scoop above is the only pickup effect: a lift toward the hero and a shrink into the pack, with the aura swelling once. No screen flash.
- **A signature per class, a twist per item:** give each class its own character (wands crackle and arc, rings glint along the band, amulets pulse like a heartbeat, potions swirl and slosh, scrolls shed drifting glyphs, magic weapons hum with edge-light), then give each item its own twist that is true to its effect.
- **Leaks are fine:** there are many ways to identify things in this game, so a little leak in how an item looks on the floor is welcome. Let an item's look and effect reflect what it really is, and let a sharp-eyed player be rewarded for noticing. Do not hold effects back just because an item is unidentified. Magic should still read as magic and mundane items as dull.
- **Readable and cheap:** effects must not hide the map or the monsters, must read at the game camera distance, and must stay cheap, because many items can be on screen at once. Do not retune the lighting constants; use emissive materials and small effects instead.

**Item interactions (first-pass flavor ideas).** These are the memorable combinations and uses a roguelike player loves; each should feel special. Bag of holding inside a bag of holding, or a wand of cancellation inside a bag of holding: a magical explosion, with the bags convulsing, light collapsing to a point, items scattering as void flecks and a violet-white shockwave. Unicorn horn, applied: a pearl-white spiral of light travels over the hero and draws the ailments out as grey and green wisps that dissolve; dipped to test a potion, the horn glows or clouds by the result. Lizard corpse, eaten: a cool green-grey calm washes over, confusion swirls subside, and if stoning is cured the grey stone skin cracks and falls away. Scroll of scare monster, dropped: a dark red dread seal is inked on the floor, and monsters flinch back from its edge (picked up a second time it crumbles to dust). Cockatrice corpse wielded with gloves, the rubber chicken: the corpse is held aloft with a sickly pale tint and a dark-humoured floppy swing, and a victim's skin creeps to grey stone. Polymorph zapped at a floor pile: the items churn, flicker and reform as new items; zapped at a monster or pet, its body churns, its outline blurs through shifting silhouettes, then settles. Engraving with a wand: each wand etches its own way (lightning scorches a bright flash into the floor, fire chars, digging chips, cold frosts, teleportation makes the engraving vanish). Ring dropped in a sink: it spins down the drain with a rust drip and a flash of its colour, and if the sink swallows it, a gurgle and a swirl. Towel applied to a cream-pie face: cream splatters the edges of the view, then the towel wipes it clear. Potion alchemy (dipping one potion into another): the two bottles pour and swirl together, blending colours with fizz, and the result flashes in its own colour. Recipes the player knows of: levitation plus enlightenment can make gain level (60%); fruit juice plus gain energy or gain level makes see invisible; healing plus speed makes extra healing; extra healing plus gain energy or gain level makes full healing; full healing plus gain energy or gain level makes gain ability. The engine decides every outcome; the visuals only show what it reports.

- **Tone:** this is a dark world, but it is inhabited by extremely strong magic that the player must harness to survive. Magic items are the player's lifeline and the strongest things in the world, so let their power read as vivid, intense and unmistakable against the grim backdrop: bright, strange, ancient and a little dangerous. Power is never cute, bubbly or pastel.
